Housing Shortage Explorer

Two measures of the U.S. housing shortage for 202 metropolitan areas, 2006–2024. Missing households counts the households that have not formed because people are doubling up or living with parents. The vacancy gap counts the vacant units a market lacks for normal turnover.

Missing households

The gap between expected and observed households. Expected households are calculated by applying each metro’s 2000 headship rates—the share of people who are household heads—in 40 age × race/ethnicity groups to its current population. A fixed metro-specific adjustment reconciles person-based and housing-unit-based household counts in 2000, setting the baseline gap to zero.

A positive value means fewer households than expected under this benchmark; a negative value means more. The measure does not directly identify why household formation differs from the benchmark, and a negative value does not necessarily indicate excess housing.

Age and race/ethnicity views aggregate contributions from the same joint calculation. A reconciliation adjustment is allocated across groups in proportion to their observed household counts so that the signed group contributions sum to each metro’s total, apart from rounding. Education uses a separate benchmark, described under “Data and totals.”

Vacancy gap

The difference between the housing stock needed to accommodate current households at an estimated natural vacancy rate and the existing stock in each owner or rental market. Equivalently, it measures the gap between the vacant units implied by that benchmark and observed vacancies, holding occupied units fixed.

Natural vacancy rates are estimated for each metro and tenure market from the relationship between vacancy rates and subsequent house-price or rent growth.

A positive value indicates fewer vacant units than the benchmark implies; a negative value indicates more. The measure describes vacancy conditions for existing households and does not include the additional housing demand represented by missing households.

Data and totals

Estimates use the 2000 Census 5% sample and ACS 1-year microdata from IPUMS USA for 202 metropolitan areas in the contiguous United States, using 2013 CBSA definitions. The series covers 2006–2024; 2020 estimates use the Census Bureau’s experimental ACS 1-year weights. These are preliminary research estimates for the covered metros, not a complete U.S. total.

“All 202 metros” sums positive gaps for the selected measure or group, without offsetting them against negative gaps elsewhere. For the combined vacancy gap, positive owner and rental gaps are summed separately. Because this rule is applied separately to each group, aggregate group totals need not sum to the overall total.

Percentages divide the selected gap by the metro’s 2000 reference housing stock. For “All 202 metros,” the denominator is the combined reference stock of all 202 metros. Group percentages use this same denominator, not the group’s population or household count.

Education estimates cover adults ages 25–64 and use a separate age × education headship benchmark. They are not a decomposition of the main age × race/ethnicity estimate.

Estimates are preliminary and may change as the paper is revised. Small metros and small groups carry sampling error from the ACS. Contact: Seongmoon Cho.
